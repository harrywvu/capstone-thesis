\section{Review of Related Literature}
\label{Review of Related Literature}

\subsection{Introduction}

Flood-evacuation planning connects hazard assessment, road access, shelter assignment, and human movement. This review examines how these concerns have been represented in geospatial systems and identifies the methods most relevant to a city-level planning tool.

\subsection{Flood-Aware Road Accessibility and Geospatial Decision Support}

Li et al.'s review of urban flood evacuation research places hazard assessment before shelter selection, route development, and movement analysis \cite{li2025}. Alabbad et al. showed in two Iowa cities that inundation can disconnect roads and bridges, lengthen journeys, and change the nearest reachable emergency facility \cite{alabbad2024}. Together, these studies establish why route analysis must use the access conditions of a flood scenario rather than the ordinary road network alone.

GIS studies in Thailand and Nepal combine flood-susceptibility mapping with route and destination analysis \cite{suwanno2023,parajuli2023}. Parajuli et al. also accounted for the effects of slope and flooding on walking speed and compared their susceptibility map with satellite observations. In the Philippines, Bolanio et al. assessed the suitability of existing evacuation centers in Las Nieves and presented the results through a web map \cite{bolanio2023}. Abuzo et al. compared road conditions under normal and flood scenarios in Cagayan de Oro \cite{abuzo2024}. These cases show the value of examining destinations and their connecting routes together, using local geographic evidence.

Dynamic-route studies address changes that a static map cannot represent. Ogawa et al. weighted roads according to present and projected inundation in a Japanese evacuation simulation \cite{ogawa2023}; Jeon et al. recalculated pedestrian routes when a flooded path or destination became unavailable \cite{jeon2023}. Their predictive and real-time functions require hazard data and validation beyond a static road-closure scenario. OverFlow uses modeled exposure to inform planner review and excludes a road from routing when the planner confirms its closure.

\subsection{Shelter Capacity and Population Allocation}

Shelter choice involves more than proximity. Qiu et al. combined flood-risk assessment, shelter selection, and evacuation simulation, reporting improved outcomes for their modeled shelter arrangement \cite{qiu2024}. Sharbaf et al. treated population exposure, shelter risk, and route risk as parts of one shelter-network problem \cite{sharbaf2025}. Wang et al. compared routes and shelters under different road-risk and pedestrian-demand priorities \cite{wang2024}. These studies show that a nearby facility may be unsuitable because of exposure, access, or limited space.

A Philippine location-routing model by de Veluz et al. jointly considered facilities, routes, capacities, travel time, and vehicles across typhoon scenarios \cite{develuz2023}. Gama et al. examined how shelter openings, evacuation orders, and assignments change over time as flood and road conditions develop \cite{gama2016}. Both studies demonstrate that assignments depend on the conditions and priorities of a particular scenario.

Other work examines who can reach a shelter and whether enough space is available. Bera et al. found that existing shelters in an Indian case could not serve all households within the tested travel-time limits \cite{bera2023}. Rendana et al. showed that areas classified as low flood risk are not necessarily suitable shelter sites once road access and other conditions are considered \cite{rendana2025}. Yin et al. examined the transfer needs of older residents in coastal cities, while Janatabadi and Ermagun identified communities with high flood risk and low shelter access \cite{yin2024,janatabadi2025}. Chen et al. explicitly recorded demand that existing shelters could not accommodate \cite{chen2021}.

For OverFlow, the initial assignment rule is deliberately explicit: evacuees are assigned only to reachable centers with available capacity, and remaining demand is reported. The literature identifies additional concerns, including shelter risk and mobility needs, that require local data and defined rules before inclusion in the allocation method.

\subsection{Evacuation Simulation and Related Systems}

Aketa et al. provided an early example of linking flood and road-network analysis in a GIS evacuation system \cite{aketa2007}. Mizumura et al. later compared routes that leave inundated areas quickly with ordinary shortest paths, showing that shorter distance and lower flood exposure can lead to different choices \cite{mizumura2024}. The U.S. Army Corps of Engineers' LifeSim represents a more detailed approach, combining warnings, population movement, traffic, flood exposure, and potential life loss \cite{lifesim2021}. These systems illustrate a progression from route calculation to dynamic evacuation modeling, with increasing demands for behavioral and hazard evidence.

Gonz\'alez-Villa et al. combined GIS, network analysis, and pedestrian and vehicle simulation in a wildfire evacuation system \cite{gonzalezvilla2022}. Du et al. coupled flood, agent-based, and traffic models to compare shelter arrangements and routing strategies; their results showed that shelter distribution and system-level routing can substantially affect modeled clearance time \cite{du2023}. These systems demonstrate the value of comparing complete arrangements, while their traffic and behavior models require data that a simpler scenario tool does not assume.

Studies of data-constrained and coastal settings reinforce the importance of stating what a simulation represents. Butters and Dawson tested shelter locations, crossings, and population samples in Kibera and found that a very small sample produced unstable risk estimates \cite{butters2025}. Yang et al. examined warning time, infrastructure failure, and shelter capacity in Shanghai \cite{yang2025}. Bustami et al. showed how compound hazards and local shelter placement changed modeled outcomes in Connecticut \cite{bustami2026}. Their numerical results belong to those modeled settings; the transferable lesson is to compare alternatives under documented inputs and examine local as well as citywide effects.

Evacuation also places demands on vehicles and post-arrival supplies. Santana-Robles et al. linked shelter assignment with aid delivery after a Mexican flood \cite{santana2024}. Woo and Kang combined bus evacuation, congestion, shelter selection, and relief routing \cite{woo2025}. Insani et al. modeled vehicle reuse, uncertain demand, and relief delivery before inundation reached affected areas \cite{insani2024}. These studies motivate recording resource assumptions alongside routes and center assignments, without treating a simplified vehicle estimate as a traffic or logistics simulation.

\subsection{Terrain Processing and Evacuation Behavior}

O'Callaghan and Mark's D8 method derives drainage directions and contributing area from gridded elevation data \cite{ocallaghan1984}. It can provide a terrain reference, but flood depth and timing require a flood model. OverFlow uses SFINCS for rainfall-driven scenarios, with its inputs, forcing, and outputs documented by Deltares \cite{sfincsinput,sfincsdocs,sfincsoutput}.

Observed behavior also differs from a mathematically shortest path. Lim et al. found that route choice among flood-affected households in Quezon City was influenced by hazard, socioeconomic, and evacuation factors \cite{lim2019}. Borowska-Stefańska et al. showed that self-evacuation can redistribute traffic well beyond a flood zone \cite{borowskastefanska2023}. OverFlow's routes are planning options under stated access and travel assumptions, rather than predictions of individual movement or congestion.

\subsection{Synthesis and Research Gap}

The literature establishes strong methods for flood mapping, road analysis, shelter assessment, and evacuation simulation, but these functions are often studied separately or within data-intensive models. Philippine studies provide evidence on route behavior, center suitability, flooded roads, and location-routing decisions \cite{lim2019,bolanio2023,abuzo2024,develuz2023}. The reviewed local applications do not combine these concerns in one city-level interface for comparing flood and operational scenarios.

OverFlow addresses this gap by bringing flood-scenario information, confirmed road closures, evacuation demand, reachable-center capacity, and resource assumptions into one planning workflow. Its evaluation examines the flood model, route and assignment constraints, system functions, and staff use as distinct questions. This structure allows the study to report what each component demonstrates without requiring readers to treat a route calculation as proof of road safety or an estimated travel time as a prediction of actual evacuation behavior.
